The biggest risk is that "helps" is vague. Contrastive pretraining could beat a shallow detector on a benchmark and still tell you nothing about the contrastive part. The design below is built to isolate that part. A few answers from you would sharpen it.

## Questions that would change the design

1. **Is the setting unsupervised (train on unlabeled or mostly-normal data) or semi-supervised (a few labeled anomalies)?** This decides which baselines are fair. It also decides how you can tune hyperparameters without leaking test labels.
2. **What does "helps" mean?** Does it mean better than the best classical detector, better than the same network without pretraining, or better in a low-data or noisy-data regime? These are three different claims with three different comparisons.
3. **Which datasets, and what compute?** Do you plan to use a public benchmark suite or your own domain data? How many seeds and datasets can you afford?
4. **What is the anomaly type?** Are they point anomalies in the feature space, or violations of dependencies between features? Contrastive augmentations behave very differently in each case.

## Core design: isolate the pretraining

The claim is about contrastive pretraining specifically. So the central comparison holds the architecture, the downstream detector, and the data fixed, and varies only the pretraining objective.

**Variables**
- Independent: representation source (none, other pretraining objectives, contrastive).
- Held fixed: encoder architecture and size, the scoring head, the training data, the tuning budget.
- Dependent: detection performance, ideally in more than one regime (full data, small data, contaminated training data).

**Baselines, from cheapest to most telling**

| Tier | Baseline | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Lower bound | Raw features into a simple detector | The floor |
| Classical | Isolation Forest, LOF, kNN distance, ECOD/COPOD, one-class SVM | Tabular data is where shallow methods are famously hard to beat. If you lose to these, that is the headline result. |
| Same network, no pretraining | Randomly initialized encoder, trained with the same downstream objective | Isolates "pretraining" from "deep model" |
| Same network, different pretraining | Autoencoder or masked-feature reconstruction, and a self-supervised pretext-task method (e.g. predicting transformations) | Isolates "contrastive" from "any pretraining". This is the most important comparison. |
| Deep AD | Deep SVDD-style one-class objective, a neural-transformation approach | The deep methods a reviewer will ask about |
| Contrastive, no pretraining stage | The same contrastive loss trained jointly or only on the downstream objective | Separates the loss from the two-stage recipe |

Two things to check about the baselines. First, they need a tuning budget equal to your method's. Under-tuned baselines are the most common way these papers mislead. Second, count parameters and training compute. If the contrastive model is larger or trained longer, match the baselines or report the difference.

| Row | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Full method | Reference |

Augmentation and negative-sampling choices interact, so vary them in a small grid rather than one at a time if you can afford it. If compute is tight, the three rows I'd keep are random init, reconstruction pretraining, and the augmentation variation.

## Evaluation: where these experiments usually go wrong

**Fatal risks**
- **Test labels used for tuning.** In unsupervised AD there are no validation labels, so people tune the augmentation, the epochs, or the score threshold on the test set. Fix: pick one protocol up front. Either use fixed hyperparameters across all datasets, or tune on a held-out split with synthetic or labeled anomalies that never touch the test set. Apply the same rule to the baselines.
- **Preprocessing leakage.** Fit scalers, encoders, and any feature selection on training data only. Anomalies must not appear in the pretraining set (or, if you test robustness to contamination, make the contamination rate explicit and identical across methods).

**Serious risks**
- **Single seeds and best-run reporting.** Differences between detectors on one dataset are often within seed variance. Run at least 5 seeds, report mean and spread, and avoid cherry-picking the best epoch.
- **Dataset choice.** Many public tabular AD datasets are small, and several are nearly saturated, so almost every method scores similarly. Use many datasets (a few dozen if possible), report per-dataset results, and use average rank with a significance test rather than only mean AUROC. Be wary of datasets where "anomalies" are just a rare class of a classification problem. Those can mislead about real anomaly detection.
- **Metric mismatch.** AUROC can look good while precision at the operating point is poor, especially at low anomaly rates. Report AUPRC as well, and precision at k if you have a deployment story.
- **Whether the gain is about pretraining or about data.** Test across training-set sizes. A plausible, publishable finding is that contrastive pretraining helps mostly when data is scarce or contaminated, and does nothing when data is plentiful.

**Minor**
- Report training time and inference cost. A small gain at 100x the cost is a different result.
- State the scoring function explicitly (distance to a centroid, kNN in embedding space, density). It matters as much as the encoder.

## When I'd say "reconsider"

If your plan is only "contrastive model vs. Isolation Forest on a handful of datasets", I'd push back. That design can't tell you whether contrastive pretraining is the cause of any difference. The design above fixes that. If you lose to Isolation Forest on most datasets, that is a real result rather than a failure. It would be a negative finding with a clear scope.

**Cheap pilot (a day or two):** pick 5 to 8 varied datasets, and run only three arms: Isolation Forest, a random-init encoder, and a contrastively pretrained encoder, with 5 seeds. If the contrastive arm doesn't beat the random-init arm, the main claim is in trouble before you spend on the full grid. If it does, scale up to the baselines and ablations above.
