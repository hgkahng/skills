## A few questions first

These change the design, so answers will sharpen everything below.

1. **What is the anomaly setting?** Fully unsupervised (training data is unlabeled and possibly contaminated), semi-supervised one-class (training data is clean normal only), or do you have a few labeled anomalies? "Does pretraining help" has a different answer in each.
2. **What does "helps" mean?** Better AUROC/AUPRC with the same downstream detector, better sample efficiency, better robustness to training contamination, or something else? "Pretraining" also needs pinning down: pretrain on the same unlabeled training set, or on a separate larger corpus of tables?
3. **Which datasets, and what kind of anomalies?** Real labeled benchmarks (where "anomaly" is often just a minority class), or synthetic injection? This matters a lot, see below.
4. **Constraints:** how many datasets, how much compute, how much time?

## Where I think the biggest risks are

**Fatal**

- **Missing the obvious strong baselines.** On tabular data, simple shallow detectors (kNN distance, Isolation Forest, PCA reconstruction, ECOD/COPOD-style marginal methods, LOF, one-class SVM) are famously hard to beat, and deep methods often lose to them. A reviewer might argue that if your contrastive model doesn't clearly beat kNN or Isolation Forest on raw features, the claim "contrastive pretraining helps anomaly detection" isn't supported, however much it helps relative to a deep baseline. Your headline comparison should be against these, not just against an autoencoder.
- **No "same detector, with and without pretraining" control.** The causal question is about pretraining, so the cleanest design holds the downstream detector fixed (say kNN or a Gaussian/Mahalanobis score on the embedding) and varies only the representation: raw features vs. random-init encoder vs. contrastively pretrained encoder. If you only compare end-to-end methods, you can't attribute any gain to pretraining as opposed to the detector, architecture, or tuning.
- **Tuning leakage in unsupervised AD.** There are no labels to tune on in the real setting. If you pick hyperparameters, augmentations, or early-stopping epochs by looking at test AUROC, the result is inflated. Decide up front: fixed hyperparameters across all datasets, or tuning only on a held-out split that's disjoint from test, and apply the same protocol to every baseline.

**Serious**

- **Augmentation is the whole game for tabular contrastive learning.** Images have natural augmentations (crop, color jitter); tabular data doesn't. Whatever you use (feature masking, noise, swapping values from other rows, mixup-style, or learned/neighbor-based positives) defines what the representation becomes invariant to. If an augmentation destroys exactly the feature interactions that distinguish anomalies, pretraining will hurt. This is the key design variable, so sweep it deliberately rather than picking one.
- **Anomaly type interacts with the method.** Local anomalies, clustered anomalies, global/outlying anomalies, and dependency anomalies (individually normal feature values, abnormal combination) favor different detectors. If your datasets are mostly one type, your conclusion will quietly be about that type. Report results stratified by dataset or anomaly type where you can.
- **Seed variance and small effect sizes.** Many tabular AD benchmarks are small, with a handful of anomalies, so AUROC is noisy. Use multiple seeds (at least 5-10) for both pretraining and the data split, report mean and spread, and avoid reporting the best run. Use a rank-based comparison across datasets (e.g., critical-difference style or paired tests) rather than eyeballing averages.
- **Metric choice.** AUROC can look great at tiny anomaly rates while being useless operationally. Report AUPRC (or precision@k) as well, and note the anomaly rate per dataset.
- **Contamination.** If the unlabeled pretraining data contains anomalies, contrastive learning may pull them into the normal manifold. Contamination robustness is a good secondary experiment: vary the contamination rate (0%, 1%, 5%, 10%) and see whether pretraining degrades more or less than the baselines.

**Minor**

- Preprocessing parity: categorical encoding, scaling, and handling of missing values should be identical across methods and fit on training data only.
- Compute and parameter matching: a pretrained deep encoder plus a detector uses far more compute than Isolation Forest. Report it, so the reader knows what the gain costs.
- Report a lower bound (random-init encoder) and not only an upper-bound framing.

## Suggested baselines

Group them so each one answers a specific question.

1. **Raw-feature shallow detectors (the real competition):** kNN distance, Isolation Forest, LOF, PCA reconstruction error, one-class SVM, and a marginal/density-style method such as ECOD or COPOD. The PyOD library has implementations of all of these, which also helps reproducibility.
2. **Same detector on learned-but-not-contrastive features:** an autoencoder bottleneck (or denoising autoencoder) embedding, and a **random-init encoder** embedding with the same architecture as your contrastive one. The random encoder is the control for "does the architecture alone do this?" It's cheap and reviewers like it.
3. **Other self-supervised objectives:** a masked-feature-prediction pretext task and, if relevant, a classic one-class deep method such as Deep SVDD, or a transformation-prediction approach (e.g., a "predict which random transformation was applied" style task). These test whether *contrastive* is the helpful ingredient, or just *any* self-supervised pretraining.
4. **Contrastive variants (the ablations of your own method):** augmentation type, with and without negatives (or a non-contrastive variant), embedding dimension, and a different downstream scorer on the same embedding (kNN vs. Mahalanobis vs. Isolation Forest on embeddings).

If you have labeled anomalies at all, add a simple supervised or semi-supervised baseline (gradient-boosted trees on the labeled subset) so readers know what the upper end looks like.

## Minimal ablation table

Rows (all with the same downstream scorer, same seeds):

| Representation | What it isolates |
|---|---|
| Raw features | No-representation floor |
| Random-init encoder | Architecture effect only |
| Autoencoder embedding | Non-contrastive learned features |
| Masked-feature self-supervision | Different pretext task |
| Contrastive, augmentation A / B / C | Effect of the augmentation choice |
| Contrastive, no negatives | Are negatives doing anything? |

If compute is tight, the three I'd treat as non-negotiable are: raw features vs. random-init vs. contrastive (with the same scorer), the strongest shallow detector run end to end, and at least two augmentation choices. Note the interaction: the best scorer (kNN vs. Mahalanobis) may depend on the augmentation, so don't fix both blindly. Pick the scorer on a validation protocol, not on test.

## Experimental protocol sketch

- **Datasets:** a mix of real tabular AD benchmarks with varied sizes, dimensionality, and anomaly rates, plus a small controlled synthetic suite where you control anomaly type and contamination. The synthetic suite is where you can make causal claims; the real ones show whether it matters in practice.
- **Splits:** fit everything (scalers, encoders, detectors) on the training split only; evaluate on a held-out split containing both classes. Repeat across seeds.
- **Metrics:** AUROC and AUPRC, mean and standard deviation over seeds, plus average rank across datasets.
- **Success criterion, decided in advance:** for instance, "contrastive embedding + kNN beats raw kNN and the best shallow detector on average rank, with a paired test across datasets." If the likely outcome is "ties kNN on raw features," say what you'd conclude from that, because it's a real possibility.

## Honest assessment

I'd put real odds on the outcome being "pretraining helps on some dataset types and not others, and the best shallow detector is competitive overall." That's not a reason to skip the experiment, but it's a reason to frame it as *when* pretraining helps (anomaly type, dimensionality, contamination, sample size) rather than *whether*. A cheap pilot before the full sweep: on 3-5 datasets, run raw kNN vs. random-init encoder + kNN vs. one contrastive variant + kNN with a few seeds. If the contrastive embedding doesn't beat raw kNN there, the full experiment is unlikely to rescue the headline claim, and that tells you to pivot toward the contamination or low-data angle.
