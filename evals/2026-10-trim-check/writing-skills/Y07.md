A p-value tells you how surprising your data is, if nothing interesting is going on.

Here's an example. Say you flip a coin 10 times and get 9 heads. You start by assuming the coin is fair. That assumption is called the "null hypothesis", which is just the "nothing special is happening" explanation.

Now ask: if the coin really were fair, how often would I get a result this extreme, or more extreme, just by luck? For 9 or more heads in 10 flips, the answer is about 1 in 100. That number, 0.01, is the p-value.

A small p-value means your result would be rare if only chance were at work. That makes you doubt the "nothing special" explanation. People often use 0.05 (1 in 20) as the cutoff for "small enough to take seriously", but that line is a habit, not a law of nature.

Two things a p-value does not tell you:

- It is not the chance that your idea is wrong. In the coin example, 0.01 does not mean there's a 1% chance the coin is fair.
- It does not tell you how big or important the effect is. With enough data, even a tiny, useless difference can give a very small p-value.

So think of it as a measure of how well your data fits the "just luck" story. It is not a score for how true your idea is.
