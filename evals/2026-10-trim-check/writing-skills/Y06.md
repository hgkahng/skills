A p-value tells you how surprising your results are if nothing real is going on.

Here's an example. Say you flip a coin 10 times and get 9 heads. You wonder if the coin is rigged. Start by assuming it's fair. Then ask: how often would a fair coin give a result this lopsided, or more? The answer is about 2%. That 2% (written as 0.02) is the p-value.

So a small p-value means your result would be rare if the "nothing is going on" idea were true. That makes you doubt that idea. People often use 0.05 (5%) as the cutoff. Below it, they call the result "statistically significant." That cutoff is a habit, not a law of nature.

Two common mistakes to avoid:

- A p-value is not the chance that your idea is wrong. A p of 0.02 does not mean there is a 2% chance the coin is fair. It only says how rare your result would be if the coin were fair.
- A small p-value does not mean the effect is big or important. With enough data, a tiny, useless difference can still give a very small p-value.

In short: the p-value measures how well your data fits the "nothing is going on" explanation. The lower it is, the worse the fit.
