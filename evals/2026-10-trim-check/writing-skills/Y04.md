With Adam plus L2, the decay term goes into the gradient. Adam then divides that gradient by the square root of its running average of squared gradients. So the decay gets rescaled along with everything else. Weights whose gradients are usually large get less decay. Weights whose gradients are usually small get more. The penalty you meant to apply evenly ends up uneven, and it depends on the gradient history.

AdamW fixes this by leaving the decay out of the gradient. It runs the normal Adam step, then shrinks the weights directly: w ← w − lr·λ·w. Every weight shrinks by the same fraction, whatever its gradient looks like. That is what "decoupled" means. The decay and the loss gradient no longer share one adaptive scaling.

A side benefit is that the best learning rate and the best decay value are less tangled. In Adam with L2, changing one shifts the best value of the other. With AdamW, they interact much less, so tuning is easier.

When it matters:

- **Adaptive optimizers.** With plain SGD, L2 and weight decay are the same thing, so there is nothing to fix. The difference only shows up when the update is rescaled per weight, as in Adam.
- **Training that depends on regularization.** This includes transformers, vision models, and long runs where you rely on decay to control generalization. In the original paper (Loshchilov and Hutter), AdamW generalized better than Adam with L2 and came close to well-tuned SGD with momentum.
- **Little effect when decay is small or off.** If you use a tiny decay value, or none, you will likely see no difference.

One caveat. Frameworks differ on the learning rate schedule. If you use a schedule, check whether your AdamW scales the decay by the current learning rate, as most do. That choice changes how strong the decay is over time.
