In Adam, an L2 penalty adds a term to the gradient: the gradient becomes g + λw. Adam then divides the whole thing by the square root of its running average of squared gradients. So the decay term gets rescaled along with the loss gradient. Weights with large gradient history get less decay. Weights with small gradient history get more. The decay you actually apply is no longer λw. It depends on the optimizer's per-weight scaling, which has nothing to do with how big the weight is.

AdamW fixes this by leaving the decay out of the gradient. It does the usual Adam step on the loss gradient alone, then shrinks the weights directly: w ← w − lr·λ·w. Every weight shrinks by the same fraction, whatever its gradient history. That is the weight decay you get with plain SGD, so the decay strength is easier to reason about and tune.

For plain SGD the two methods are the same thing, so nothing changes there. The gap only shows up with adaptive methods like Adam, where the per-weight scaling gets in the way.

It matters most in these cases:

- **Tuning.** With AdamW, the best learning rate and the best λ are much less tangled. With Adam plus L2, changing one shifts the best value of the other. This was the main finding in Loshchilov and Hutter's paper.
- **Generalization.** Adam plus L2 often regularizes poorly and generalizes worse than SGD with momentum. AdamW closes much of that gap. This is why it is the default for transformers and most large models.
- **Uneven gradients.** The more the gradient sizes differ across weights, such as with embeddings or rare features, the more L2 under Adam distorts the decay.

If your gradients are small and fairly uniform, or you use little or no decay, you may not see a difference.
