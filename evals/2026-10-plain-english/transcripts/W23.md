A database index works like the index at the back of a book.

Without an index, the database has to read every row in a table to find the ones you want. If the table has 10 million rows, that's 10 million checks. This is called a full table scan.

With an index, the database keeps a second, sorted list of the values in one column, plus a pointer to where each row lives. Say you search for the email "ana@example.com". The database looks in the sorted list, jumps straight to that entry, and follows the pointer to the row. It never touches the other rows.

The sorted list is usually stored as a tree, most often a B-tree. A tree lets the database narrow things down in steps. At each step it throws away most of what's left, the same way you'd find a name in a phone book by opening to the middle and then going left or right. With 10 million rows, that takes only a few steps instead of millions of checks.

Indexes have a cost:

- **They take space.** Each index is an extra copy of some of your data.
- **They slow down writes.** When you add, change, or delete a row, the database also has to update every index on that table.

So you don't put an index on everything. You add one to the columns you often search, filter, join, or sort by, and you leave the rest alone.
