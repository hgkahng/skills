A gap that large (99% train vs. 70% validation) is the classic sign of **overfitting**: the model has memorized the training set, including its noise and quirks, instead of learning patterns that generalize. But overfitting isn't the only possible cause, so it's worth checking a few things.

## Most likely causes

1. **The model is too flexible for the amount of data.** Deep networks, unpruned decision trees, gradient boosting with many rounds, or k-NN with k=1 can fit almost any training set perfectly. If you have relatively few examples, the model can memorize them.

2. **Not enough training data, or data that isn't diverse enough.** The model sees too few variations to learn the real signal.

3. **Training too long without regularization.** Training accuracy keeps climbing while validation accuracy plateaus or drops.

4. **Too many features relative to samples.** Especially with noisy or irrelevant features, the model finds spurious correlations.

## Causes that look like overfitting but aren't

5. **Distribution shift between train and validation.** If the validation set comes from a different time period, user group, source, or class balance, the model can be fine on its own terms and still do worse. Compare feature and label distributions between the two sets.

6. **Data leakage in training.** Duplicates or near-duplicates in the training set, or features that leak the label, can inflate training accuracy (and sometimes validation, if the leak spans both). Check for duplicate rows and for features that wouldn't be available at prediction time.

7. **A preprocessing mismatch.** Normalization, tokenization, or augmentation applied differently to train and validation data (for example, scaler fit on the wrong split, or augmentation accidentally left on or off) can cause a drop.

8. **Train/eval mode bugs.** In frameworks like PyTorch, forgetting `model.eval()` (so dropout and batch norm behave as in training) or a mismatch in how metrics are computed can give misleading numbers.

9. **A small or unrepresentative validation set.** With a few hundred samples, 70% can carry a wide margin of error, and an unlucky split can look worse than it is.

## How to fix it

Start with the diagnosis, then apply remedies in roughly this order:

- **Plot learning curves** (train vs. validation loss/accuracy per epoch). If validation loss rises while training loss keeps falling, it's overfitting. Use **early stopping** at the point where validation performance peaks.
- **Get more data**, or **augment** what you have (image transforms, noise, text paraphrasing, etc.).
- **Add regularization:** L1/L2 weight decay, dropout, smaller learning rate, or for tree models, limit depth, raise `min_samples_leaf`, or subsample.
- **Reduce model capacity:** fewer layers or units, shallower trees, fewer boosting rounds.
- **Simplify the features:** drop irrelevant ones, or use feature selection or dimensionality reduction.
- **Use cross-validation** to get a more reliable estimate than a single split.
- **Try a simple baseline** (logistic regression, a small tree). If it gets about 70% on both sets, your large model is mostly memorizing.
- **Audit the pipeline** for leakage, split problems, and train/eval inconsistencies listed above.

## Quick sanity checks

- What's the class balance? 70% may be near the majority-class rate, so look at precision/recall/F1 and not just accuracy.
- Are train and validation drawn from the same distribution, with no overlap between them?
- Does the gap shrink when you shrink the model or add regularization? If so, it's overfitting.
