**Why decouple.** With Adam plus L2, you add the penalty to the loss, so the gradient becomes g + λθ. That combined term then goes through Adam's adaptive rescaling:

θ ← θ − η · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε), where m and v are built from g + λθ.

The decay is therefore divided by √v̂ along with everything else. Parameters with a large gradient history (large v̂) get little effective regularization. Parameters with small, rarely updated gradients get a lot. So the strength of the regularizer depends on the gradient statistics, not just on λ. It is no longer the "shrink every weight by the same fraction" behavior that L2 gives under plain SGD.

AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) takes the decay out of the gradient and applies it directly to the weights:

θ ← θ − η · ( m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) + λθ )

Now every weight shrinks by the same factor, η·λ, per step, whatever its gradient history. This is what weight decay means for SGD. For SGD the two forms are equivalent up to a reparameterization of λ. For adaptive methods they are not, and AdamW restores the intended behavior. Adam with L2 isn't minimizing "loss + λ‖θ‖²/2" in any clean sense, because the preconditioner distorts the penalty's gradient.

**When it matters.**

- **Tuning and generalization.** In Adam+L2, the best λ interacts with the learning rate and the gradient scale. In AdamW, the optimal λ and η are closer to separable, since the decay is scaled by η only. Hyperparameter search is easier, and the paper shows better generalization on image classification. Transformers are the setting where it is now standard practice.
- **Heterogeneous gradient scales.** The difference grows when v̂ varies a lot across parameters, as with embeddings, sparse features, and layers of very different scale. Under L2 these parameters are regularized unevenly. Under AdamW they are regularized uniformly.
- **Little effect elsewhere.** If you use plain SGD, or you don't regularize at all, there is no difference. If λ is tiny, or gradient scales are fairly uniform, the two behave similarly in practice. Also, the "wd" in many frameworks' `Adam(weight_decay=...)` is L2 coupled into the gradient, so check which one you are actually getting. In PyTorch, `torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...)` is L2 and `torch.optim.AdamW` is decoupled.

**Practical notes.**

- Typical AdamW values are λ around 0.01–0.1, much larger than typical L2 coefficients, because the decay is multiplied by η, not rescaled by Adam's preconditioner.
- Exclude biases and normalization parameters from decay. This is common practice.
- If you use a learning-rate schedule, the decay follows it, since the shrink per step is η_t·λ. The paper also proposes a separate schedule multiplier for this, which many implementations fold into η_t.

**Caveat.** The "decoupled is better" story is mostly empirical. Later work argues that AdamW's benefit is not about being a true regularizer. It may come more from controlling the effective learning rate of scale-invariant weights, for example weights followed by normalization layers. So it is worth checking the effect in your own setup, not assuming the theory fully explains it.
