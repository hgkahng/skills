A p-value tells you how surprising your results would be if nothing real were going on.

Say you flip a coin 10 times and get 9 heads. You wonder if the coin is rigged. Start by assuming it's fair. The p-value is the chance of getting a result at least this extreme, 9 or more heads (or 9 or more tails, if you count both directions), from a fair coin. That chance is about 2%, so the p-value is about 0.02.

A small p-value means your results would be rare if nothing were going on. That makes "nothing is going on" harder to believe. People often use 0.05 as the cutoff for "small enough," but that number is a habit, not a law.

Two common mistakes:

- A p-value of 0.02 does not mean there's a 2% chance the coin is fair. It only describes how the data behave if the coin is fair.
- It doesn't tell you how big or important an effect is. A tiny, useless difference can still give a small p-value if you have enough data.
