A p-value tells you how surprising your results would be if nothing interesting were going on.

Here's an example. Say you flip a coin 100 times and get 70 heads. You start by assuming the coin is fair. That assumption is called the null hypothesis. Then you ask: if the coin really were fair, how often would I get a result this lopsided, or more lopsided? For 70 or more heads, the answer is far less than 1 percent of the time. That chance is the p-value.

A small p-value means your results would be rare if the "nothing is going on" assumption were true. So it gives you a reason to doubt that assumption. People often use 0.05 (5 percent) as the cutoff. Below it, they call the result "statistically significant." That cutoff is a habit, not a law of nature.

Two common mistakes to avoid:

- A p-value is not the chance that your idea is wrong. A p-value of 0.03 does not mean there's a 3 percent chance the coin is fair. It only says how rare your data would be if the coin were fair.
- A small p-value does not mean the effect is big or important. With enough data, even a tiny, useless difference can give a small p-value.

In short, the p-value measures how well your data fits the "nothing is going on" story. It does not tell you how likely that story is to be true.
