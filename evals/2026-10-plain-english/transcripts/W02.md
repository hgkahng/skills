Because with Adam, L2 regularization and weight decay stop being the same thing, and L2 does the wrong job.

**What goes wrong with Adam plus L2.** L2 adds a term λw to the gradient. Adam then divides the whole gradient by a running estimate of its size (the square root of the second moment). So the pull toward zero gets divided too. A weight with large gradients is shrunk only a little. A weight with small gradients is shrunk a lot. The penalty ends up depending on the gradient history, which is not what you wanted from it.

**What AdamW does.** It runs the Adam step on the loss gradient alone. Then it shrinks the weights directly: w ← w − lr·λ·w. Every weight shrinks by the same fraction, no matter how big its gradients have been.

**When it matters.**
- With plain SGD, L2 and weight decay are identical, so the split never mattered there. It only matters for adaptive optimizers like Adam.
- With Adam plus L2, the weights with the largest gradients get the least regularization. Those are often the ones you most want to constrain.
- In AdamW, the best λ is less tied to the learning rate and the gradient scale. That makes tuning easier, and the decay follows your learning-rate schedule.
- The gap is biggest in long training runs of large models, such as transformers, where generalization is the goal. With a very small λ or a short run, you may see little difference.
