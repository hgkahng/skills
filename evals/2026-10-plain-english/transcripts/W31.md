A p-value is a number that tells you how surprising your data would be if there were actually no effect.

More precisely, it is the probability of seeing results at least as extreme as the ones you observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. The null hypothesis is the "nothing is going on" assumption, such as "this drug has no effect" or "these two groups have the same average."

**An example**

Say you flip a coin 100 times and get 60 heads. You want to know if the coin is biased. Assume it's fair (the null hypothesis). Then ask: if the coin really were fair, how often would I get 60 or more heads, or an equally lopsided result in the other direction (40 or fewer)? That probability is about 0.057. That's your p-value.

**How to read it**

- A small p-value (commonly below 0.05) means your data would be unlikely under the null hypothesis. That is taken as evidence against the null.
- A large p-value means your data are consistent with the null. It does not prove the null is true. It only means you lack strong evidence against it.

The 0.05 cutoff is a convention, not a law of nature. Different fields use different thresholds, and a result at p = 0.049 isn't meaningfully different from one at p = 0.051.

**Common misunderstandings**

- It is **not** the probability that the null hypothesis is true. A p-value of 0.03 does not mean there's a 3% chance the result is a fluke.
- It is **not** the probability that your finding will replicate.
- It does **not** measure the size or importance of an effect. With a large enough sample, a tiny and practically meaningless effect can produce a very small p-value. Always look at the effect size and confidence interval too.
- A non-significant result is **not** proof of "no effect."

**In short:** the p-value answers "If nothing were going on, how often would I see data this extreme?" It is a useful measure of how well your data fit the null hypothesis, but it is only one piece of evidence and shouldn't be the whole story.
